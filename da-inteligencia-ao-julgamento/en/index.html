From Intelligence to Judgment

What AI still can't do on its own — and why Context Engineering decides whether it gets there, or breaks trying

Every profession has a boundary between rule-based work and instinct-based work. That boundary is migrating toward the machine — but only when an AI system can accumulate, organize and use human experience without breaking in the process. Most production AI projects fail exactly there: not because the model is weak, but because nobody maintained the context it was accumulating. This piece documents the four failure modes silently eroding AI systems, the maintenance cycle that prevents them, and why the AI infrastructure industry itself now points to data and context — not the model — as the 2026 frontier.

01 — The boundary From intelligence to judgment

Richard and Daniel Susskind, in The Future of the Professions, describe a boundary that runs through every profession: on one side, routine cognitive work — complex, but reducible to rules; on the other, judgment — experience, instinct, sensitivity to nuance, built over years of practice[1]. That boundary isn't fixed. It moves as technology advances, and the Susskinds' thesis is that it will keep migrating toward the machine — not because judgment stops existing, but because part of what we call judgment today will prove, with enough data, more mappable than it looked.

"Today's judgement will become tomorrow's intelligence." Julien Bek, Sequoia Capital — 2026

That's a recent, specific way of articulating the same idea[2]. The logic is simple to state: as an AI system accumulates proprietary data about how human experts decide — the history of corrections, the reasoning behind each choice, the specific context of each case — it starts recognizing patterns that used to take years of practice to see. What was instinct gradually becomes recognizable rule.

The problem is that this migration doesn't happen automatically. It depends on a specific technical capability: accumulating, organizing and using this growing volume of human experience without the system breaking in the process. And that's exactly where most production AI projects fail — not because the model is weak, but because nobody maintained the context it was accumulating.

02 — The incident Why it breaks without maintenance

In November 2025, a production agent pipeline — two LangChain agents exchanging messages with each other, no budget cap, no stop condition — entered a loop and ran for 11 straight days. Nobody noticed until the bill arrived. Stanford researchers, looking at the problem more broadly, measured just how far this unpredictability can scale in common agentic tasks[3].

This isn't a weak-model problem. It's a badly managed context problem — the same kind of disorganized information buildup that, instead of teaching the system judgment, makes it lose the ability to solve even the routine tasks it already mastered. Context Engineering — as a discipline of continuous maintenance, not a one-time architecture decision — exists to prevent exactly this kind of breakdown. It isn't just good operating practice: it's the infrastructure that decides whether the intelligence-to-judgment crossing described above ends in real capability or in expensive, silent collapse.

03 — The definition What is Context Engineering

Context Engineering is the art and science of providing the right information, in the right format, at the right time, so a language model can plausibly solve a task[4]. The term was popularized by Tobi Lütke, CEO of Shopify, who described it as "the art of providing all the context for the task to be plausibly solvable by the LLM"[5].

The concept goes beyond prompt engineering — which handles only the formatting of instructions — to take on the dynamic management of the model's entire context window: conversation history, available tools, real-time retrieved data, and knowledge of the environment the agent operates in. Anthropic describes the discipline's central problem as "optimizing the utility of those tokens against the inherent constraints of LLMs, in order to consistently achieve a desired outcome"[5].

The point that usually goes unnoticed: this isn't an architecture choice made once, at system design time. An agent that works well in month 1 can silently degrade by month 6 — precisely the volume of experience that, well managed, should be teaching the system judgment.

04 — The diagnosis The four failure modes

The technical literature on the topic, synthesizing research from Google DeepMind, Microsoft and Salesforce, documents four distinct failures — each with numerical thresholds that serve as production monitoring triggers[4].

01 — POISONING

Context poisoning

A hallucination enters the conversation history and gets referenced as fact, reinforcing an incorrect goal across multiple turns. Formally studied by DeepMind on the Gemini 2.5 agent. Doesn't resolve on its own — requires active detection, because it compounds.

02 — DISTRACTION

Context distraction

Very large context windows make the model over-emphasize repeated or irrelevant information. The effect intensifies from ~100k tokens on Gemini Pro, and as early as ~32k tokens on smaller models like LLaMA 3 405B.

03 — CONFUSION

Context confusion

Too many available tools make the model call the wrong one. A quantized LLaMA 3 failed with 46 simultaneous tools, but performed well with 19. Practical guidance: keep 10 to 15 active tools per agent.

04 — CLASH

Context clash

Conflicting instructions in the same window produce inconsistent outputs — the most severe of the four failures. Fragmenting instructions across multiple turns, instead of one coherent prompt, dropped accuracy from 98% to 64% in one test.

05 — The prevention The maintenance cycle: five techniques, two categories

Context engineering uses five techniques — not three, as simplified takes on the topic tend to present it. They split into architecture techniques, decided once when the system is built, and continuous maintenance techniques, executed on every interaction, throughout the system's entire operating life.

Architecture (initial setup)

01
RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation)

Filters and injects only the knowledge strictly relevant to the current decision, instead of overloading the model with everything available.

02
Context quarantine and multi-agent systems

Splits information into isolated lanes for specialized agents, preventing conflicting instructions from coexisting in the same space — the structural defense against context clash.

03
Context offloading / memory systems

Stores knowledge outside the immediate window, allowing long-term retention without exhausting the token limit.

Continuous maintenance (day-to-day operation)

04
Pruning

Actively removes outdated or irrelevant information from the context window as the conversation progresses. Mature RAG systems do this in multiple stages.

05
Summarization

As the token limit approaches, compresses accumulated history into condensed representations. Requires careful control — poorly calibrated, it reintroduces distraction and confusion.

The first three techniques keep the system from starting out badly designed. The last two are what keeps a well-designed system from rotting with use — and, not by coincidence, they're the same two that decide whether experience accumulated over time turns into useful judgment or into noise.

06 — The practice Operational maintenance checklist

For whoever is operating — not just building — an AI system in production, context engineering maintenance should be treated as an observability routine, with the following recurring checkpoints:

01
Measure token consumption growth

Per session/task over time. Non-linear growth is a sign of a loop or of context buildup without pruning.

02
Audit the number of active tools

Exposed to each agent; keep 10 to 15, with the rest available on demand through routing.

03
Set a budget cap with automatic cutoff — not just an alert

The $47,000 case happened precisely because a budget alert was configured, but no automatic cutoff mechanism executed it[3].

04
Periodically review the agent's decision history

Looking for signs of poisoning — goals incorrectly fixed from an earlier hallucination nobody corrected.

05
Test fragmented versus consolidated instructions

The performance gap between multiple turns and a single coherent prompt measures how vulnerable your system is to context clash.

07 — The next move The third pillar: why data is the 2026 frontier

The industry tends to describe its own infrastructure in three layers: compute, model and data. That's the same split guiding the $1.7 billion fund Andreessen Horowitz dedicated to AI infrastructure in February 2026 — the firm's stated thesis is to invest across three core pillars: "compute innovation, data infrastructure, and foundational model development"[6]. The first two pillars have already had their cycle of capital and attention: 2023 and 2024 were dominated by the race for compute power; 2024 and 2025, by the race among model labs.

What the market's own investors point to as the 2026 frontier is the third pillar — and that's exactly what this piece describes.

"The limiting factor for AI companies is now data entropy: the steady decay of freshness, structure and truth inside the unstructured universe where 80% of corporate knowledge now lives." Jennifer Li, Andreessen Horowitz — Big Ideas 2026

Jason Cui, from the same team, is more direct about the mechanism: the industry's central problem in 2026 is "how AI agents navigate 'the context problem': continuously accessing the right data context and semantic layers in order to build robust applications"[7].

In other words: the same industry that funded the race for model and compute is now pointing to where this piece already was — data and context, not the model itself, are the bottleneck that decides whether an AI system works in production.

Conclusion The professional who moves up a layer alongside the AI

The crossing from intelligence to judgment isn't an event — it's a continuous process, and the fuel for that process is exactly the kind of context this piece described: decision history, corrections, nuances accumulated over time. A system that mismanages that context doesn't just fail to advance toward judgment — it regresses, losing precision even on the routine tasks it already mastered. The four failure modes documented here aren't minor technical obstacles: they're exactly what separates an AI that accumulates experience from one that accumulates noise.

"The LLM is like the CPU, and its context window is like RAM." Andrej Karpathy, one of OpenAI's original architects

Just as no one would let a CPU run with random garbage loaded into RAM, you can't expect an AI system to perform reliably without someone deliberately engineering what goes into that window[8].

That someone already has a job title: Context Engineer. The title started appearing in serious AI discussions in late 2025; by early 2026, Gartner had already published a formal definition of the discipline — "designing and structuring the relevant data, workflows and environment so AI systems can understand intent, make better decisions and deliver contextual, enterprise-aligned outcomes, without relying on manual prompts." The market already recognizes this competency as its own specialization within AI engineering, distinct from both the generalist and the earlier "prompt engineer" — with pay consistently above the field average, even though the exact range varies widely by country, company and seniority[8].

The most important detail, though, isn't the pay — it's what Gartner itself recommends to companies: appoint a context engineering lead or team integrated with AI's operational governance, not just with the technical team[8]. That ties the competency described in this piece directly to business strategy: the most valuable profile in this role isn't someone who just knows how to configure a RAG pipeline — it's someone who understands the business problem deeply enough to know what information the model actually needs to decide well. Domain knowledge matters more here than most expect.

That closes this piece's central argument. The crossing from intelligence to judgment only happens if context is managed with discipline — and whoever manages that discipline isn't a backstage technician, but the person who gets to decide, based on the business, what the AI should remember, forget and prioritize. That's Ethan Mollick's argument in Co-Intelligence: human value shifts toward supervision and judgment over what the AI produces, not toward competing with it on execution speed[9].

In 2026 practice, that shift already has a job description, a pay range, and a reserved seat at the table where strategy gets decided — not just where it gets executed. The bar moves on both sides at once: AI climbs in capability on top of the data pillar, and the professional who manages that pillar climbs a layer alongside it.
