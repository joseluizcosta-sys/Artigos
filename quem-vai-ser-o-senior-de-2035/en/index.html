Who will be the experts of 2035?

AI absorbed part of the work that used to train specialists — how to keep young professionals exposed to expert human judgment

For decades, training a specialist was an automatic by-product of the entry-level job. Nobody had to design it: the junior did the repetitive work, made mistakes next to someone who knew better, and in three or four years had become a different person. The mechanism was invisible because it ran on its own. Generative AI absorbed part of that work from 2023 onwards — and the available evidence is more nuanced, and more interesting, than the headlines suggest.

01 — The originThe first rung

Entry-level work always meant low pay, repetitive tasks and little prestige. Document review, code debugging, spreadsheet checking, ticket triage, data gathering. It was the condition for entering the labour market, and everyone understood it as a condition.

Except it was never only a condition. The repetitive work of early career was the vehicle for a transfer that nobody ever put in a contract: the novice watched the expert decide, made mistakes on a scale where mistakes were cheap, and absorbed what no manual contained. Judgment on exceptions. When to distrust the data. Which rules exist on paper and which ones hold in practice.

That content has a name in the economics literature — tacit knowledge — and two inconvenient properties: it is embodied in the person and it is not verifiable by third parties[3]. You cannot write "you will transfer your tacit knowledge to me" into a contract and enforce delivery. It passes through proximity or it does not pass at all.

The extreme case: tier-one support

Tier one is the first layer of customer support — whoever takes the contact before any specialist. It answers frequent questions, checks order status, makes simple account changes, and escalates to tier two anything requiring technical knowledge or authority. It is scripted work by design, with controlled handle time and low autonomy. And for decades it was the entry door for an entire generation of supervisors, quality monitors and operations managers.

What the official data shows — and what it doesn't. The Bureau of Labor Statistics records 2.81 million customer service representatives in the United States in 2024 and projects a 5% decline through 2034, a net loss of 153,700 positions, explicitly attributed to the automation of simple tasks by self-service systems and apps[4]. But the same document carries the counterweight: even in decline, the occupation is projected to open about 341,700 positions per year over the decade, all to replace people who leave. The layer shrinks; it does not close.

What reported journalism documents. A Bloomberg story from July 2026 names the target precisely: "Jobs focused on this simplest aspect of customer support — often called tier one — are on the chopping block, while companies are expected to retain workers trained to handle more complex interactions"[5]. The piece documents Microsoft trimming its customer service workforce from roughly 50,000 to 40,000 across staff and contractors — with Judson Althoff stating in April that AI saves the company around $750 million a year — and Brinks Home going from about 800 to 400 positions after cutting call volume by two thirds, partly through reassignment and mostly through natural attrition. Forrester analyst Kate Leggett estimates that almost half of customer service roles will be impacted by 2030. Impacted, not eliminated — the distinction tends to disappear in the retelling.

The caveat the industry would rather not hear, from the same story: salespeople at several technology companies told Bloomberg that they routinely pitch call-centre AI tools as a way of lowering labour costs — which undercuts the common claim that AI primarily exists to make workers more productive.

Why that rung mattered

Enrique Ide, an economist at IESE Business School, published a model in 2025 that formalises the intergenerational transmission of knowledge at work[3]. The structure is simple: at any moment two groups coexist inside the firm — those still learning and those who already know. Production requires expert knowledge and the execution of routine tasks, and the latter can be performed by novices or by machines. In performing routine tasks, the novice interacts with an expert, observes their skill and absorbs it. It is the formalisation of the apprenticeship arrangement: the novice accepts lower wages in exchange for the opportunity to learn.

The model's three long-run regimes

RegimeConditionOutcome
Learning breakdownExtensive automationNovice–expert interactions severely restricted, tacit knowledge eroded, stagnation
Constrained learningModerate automationSome interactions persist, positive but limited growth
Full learningExpensive automationFrequent interactions, robust transmission, maximum growth

This is not a smooth gradient. These are basins of attraction — and moving from one to another is hard to reverse, because the stock of experts capable of training is itself the result of the previous generation.

The order-of-magnitude calculation. The author derives an expression for the reduction in the steady-state growth rate and computes it with parameters from the literature: a 2% annual baseline growth rate and the premise that roughly two thirds of long-run growth comes from the diffusion of tacit knowledge. Across the two scenarios tested — 5% and 30% of entry-level tasks automated — the result is a reduction of 0.05 to 0.35 percentage points in the annual growth rate of U.S. per capita output.

What 0.35 percentage points means over half a century. It looks marginal because it is a fraction of a point. But it is 18% of baseline growth, and growth rates compound.

Half a century later, in the aggressive scenario, the economy would be roughly 16% smaller per capita than it would otherwise have been.

And why the damage hits the rate rather than the level. If automation merely removed output, the effect would be a level effect: it drops once and stops. But in the model, long-run growth comes from the diffusion of best practices — the most capable experts produce and train several novices at once, and that is how the best of what exists spreads through the economy. Breaking that channel does not cost one cohort's output; it permanently reduces the speed at which knowledge propagates. That is why the damage enters the rate, which compounds, rather than the level, which does not.

A note on the structure, because it reads oddly at first. The model parameterises a career as two stages of twenty years each: twenty learning, twenty being a source of learning for others. This does not claim that anyone spends two decades as a junior, nor that careers have only two rungs. It is a modelling simplification — a forty-year career split in half between the phase in which competence is absorbed and the phase in which it is transmitted.

Method caveats, and they are the author's own: the exercise "is neither a formal calibration nor a forecast", and "deliberately abstracts from compensating factors, such as the emergence of new junior-level roles or AI's potential to stimulate innovation". Ide also explicitly assumes that AI adoption takes the exclusive form of automation — a premise he justifies with early evidence, but a premise nonetheless.

And there is a second, more uncomfortable result: the competitive equilibrium features socially excessive automation of early-career tasks.

The term is worth unpacking, because it carries the argument. Each firm, on its own, automates the dose that makes sense for itself: the task gets cheaper, output rises, the decision is rational. What none of them pays for is the training of the specialists the whole market will hire ten years from now — because whoever trains does not necessarily retain the person trained. The benefit of training leaks to competitors; the cost of not training arrives later and is shared by everyone. Each individual decision is correct and the aggregate result is more automation than would be optimal for the economy. It is the classic structure of an externality: whoever decides does not bear the cost, which falls on third parties and on the future.

The model's most useful point for decision-makers contradicts the fatalistic reading:

The trade-off does not apply to all technological advances. Improvements in technologies that augment routine labour — that raise the novice's productivity rather than replacing the novice's work — deliver immediate productivity benefits while simultaneously reinforcing the transmission of knowledge. Ide (2025), Automation, AI, and the Intergenerational Transmission of Knowledge

Automating the novice's task erodes training. Augmenting what the novice can do does not. It is the same technology in two opposite configurations — and the choice between them is a design decision, not a technological one.

This has stopped being theory alone. The payroll study finds exactly that pattern in the data: employment declines concentrate in occupations where AI tends to automate rather than augment[2]. Theoretical model and administrative data pointing at the same variable.

The precedent that isn't about AI

The model rests on field evidence that predates generative AI, from Matt Beane's work on learning at work[11]. In surgery, the adoption of robotic systems concentrated the operation in the senior surgeon's hands and drastically reduced residents' supervised practice hours. In finance, automated tools took away from junior analysts the building work that used to train them. Beane summarises the traditional mechanism in three words — "see one, do one, teach one" — and observes that formal education delivers what he calls table stakes, the basic qualification; real competence only emerges from practice alongside someone who knows.

The practical implication: counting juniors measures the wrong thing. The operational question stops being "how many did we hire" and becomes: do the ones who are here work close to someone who knows, on problems that still require judgment?

What about mentoring programmes?

They exist, and they are common. Formal mentoring, graduate programmes, internal academies and development tracks are established practice in any sizeable company.

The question these programmes need to answer is: how many hours of real production does this professional spend alongside someone at the top of the firm's competence?

A Harvard Business Review article from July 2026 reaches the same conclusion by another route: AI is automating the very work that trained people, and the response requires redesigning mentoring — making tacit knowledge explicit and embedding learning into daily work, to replace the developmental opportunities automation removed[6].

02 — The evidenceWhat the employment data shows

Two independent studies, using administrative data rather than opinion surveys, reached converging results. In both, the divergence begins in the first quarter of 2023 — the quarter corporate adoption of generative AI begins to scale, a few months after the release of ChatGPT.

The first tracks 62 million workers across 285,000 U.S. firms between 2015 and 2025[1]. The authors identify generative-AI adoption through text analysis of job postings and compare employment by seniority against non-adopting firms.

From 2015 to 2022, adopters and non-adopters followed parallel trends. Starting in the first quarter of 2023, adopters break away: junior employment falls about 9% after six quarters relative to controls. Senior employment shows no break in trend. The decline concentrates in high-exposure roles and disappears in low-exposure ones. The mechanism is slower hiring — separations did not rise and promotions stayed stable. And the effect is strongest in the middle of the distribution: candidates from mid-tier institutions saw the steepest relative declines.

The second uses administrative data from the largest payroll software provider in the United States[2]. Workers aged 22 to 25 in the most exposed occupations experienced a 16% relative employment decline, controlling for firm-level shocks, while employment for experienced workers remained stable or kept growing. Adjustment happens through employment rather than compensation. The results survive excluding technology firms and occupations amenable to remote work.

And the declines concentrate in occupations where AI tends to automate, rather than augment, human labour — the same pattern the model in Section 01 predicts.

03 — The mechanismWhy delegating doesn't train

There is a second, individual mechanism, and on this one there is recent causal evidence. The study's framing states its case up front:

People often optimize for long-term goals in collaboration: a mentor or companion doesn't just answer questions, but also scaffolds learning, tracks progress, and prioritizes the other person's growth over immediate results. In contrast, current AI systems are fundamentally short-sighted collaborators — optimized for providing instant and complete responses, without ever saying no. Liu, Christian, Dumbalska, Bakker & Dubey (2026)

These are three randomised experiments, N = 1,222, with the second replicating the results and ruling out confounds. The finding: AI assistance improves immediate performance and, afterwards, reduces persistence and impairs unassisted performance. Assisted people give up sooner when they return to working alone. The effects emerge after roughly ten minutes of interaction[7].

And the detail that changes the recommendation: the persistence cost concentrates among those who obtain the direct solution. It is not AI use that erodes — it is receiving the finished answer. The authors explain the mechanism: AI conditions people to expect immediate answers, denying them the experience of working through difficulty on their own. And they close by noting that persistence is foundational to skill acquisition and one of the strongest predictors of long-term learning.

Method caveat: the tasks involve mathematical reasoning and reading comprehension, in short interventions in a controlled setting. Extrapolating from ten laboratory minutes to three years of professional formation is a leap the study does not authorise.

How much of real usage is the kind that substitutes for effort? Public data from the Anthropic Economic Index, for May 2026, shows observed usage split almost evenly: 51.4% classified as augmentation and 48.6% as automation[8].

Mandatory caveats: the index describes tasks, not people or jobs — the correct formulation is "AI is used for tasks commonly done by a given occupation", never "that occupation uses AI". There is no time series: it is a snapshot of one period and supports no claim about trend. And it covers one specific assistant, not the market.

And this is where the arguments meet. The augmentation–automation distinction, which in the index is a descriptive classification of usage, is in Ide's model the variable separating full learning from breakdown — and in the payroll study it is where employment declines concentrate. Three independent sources, the same dividing line.

For a trained professional, delegating is pure gain. For someone still being trained, it is the rung being skipped.

04 — The limitsConsiderations

What the researchers themselves say. The authors of the 62-million-worker study record that their findings "do not necessarily imply immediate task automation", that a plausible alternative is firms making forward-looking adjustments in response to expectations formed after the release of GPT-3.5, that "if firms have overreacted, the decline may be temporary" — and that their data does not allow them to directly test that mechanism[1].

The economic confounder was tested — and partly set aside. In a February 2026 note, the authors of the payroll study responded to the objection that interest rates, not AI, explain the decline[9]. Crossing occupational interest-rate exposure with AI exposure, they find that the most AI-exposed occupations are on average less exposed to interest rates — construction is the inverse example. And splitting occupations above and below the median of interest-rate exposure, in both groups the most AI-exposed decline more.

But the timing changes under stricter controls. With firm-time fixed effects, the decline only becomes significant in 2024 — not in late 2022 or 2023. In the authors' words, the earlier declines are "likely (at least partly) due to some combination of other factors, not just AI". The effect, on the other hand, has grown: about 16% through October 2025 against 13% with data through July, with no reversal.

Those same authors write, explicitly: "we do not believe that AI is always and everywhere the sole determinant of employment, and we do not encourage others to interpret our results in that way." And they close by acknowledging that, without an experiment comparing a world with AI to one without, the question remains open.

What companies say about themselves. In a survey of nearly a thousand U.S. business leaders conducted in February 2026, 21% name AI as the sole reason for eliminating roles, 19% as the primary driver and 26% as one of several contributing factors; 21% report having already frozen entry-level hiring because of AI, and 36% expect to have stopped by year end[10].

The same survey carries the opposite. Forty-seven per cent say they are hiring more technical or AI-focused staff, and 48% say they are hiring more workers who can effectively use AI tools. This is not pure substitution: it is a recomposition of the entry-level profile.

Caveat: this is a survey run by a commercial job-market platform, with direct interest in the narrative and no sampling detail. It supports "companies say they decided because of AI", not "companies decided because of AI".

And there is a data point pointing to expectation. In July 2026, a survey of nearly two thousand workers in the United States and the United Kingdom found that 62% believed AI was reducing entry-level hiring — but only 31% reported that it had happened in their own field. Asked about the biggest obstacle in today's market, only 9% cited AI; pay (30%) and competition (25%) came first[12]. Perception at twice the level of experience, and well above the stated priority.

The gap that bothers me most. All the evidence cited here is North American or British. I found no equivalent study using Brazilian data. Managers in Brazil are deciding about the structure of their own teams based on research conducted on a different labour market, with a different cost structure, different legislation and a different entry-level education profile.

What survives all of this is the model's argument, because it does not depend on the magnitude of the hiring decline. If training degrades when the novice stops working near the expert, uncertainty about how many juniors were hired does not change the recommendation.

05 — The responseWhat to do — and who is already doing it

First, the formative-value matrix

Every task considered for automation is assessed today on two dimensions: how much it costs and how well the machine performs it. A third is missing — how much that task trains whoever performs it. For practical use the first two collapse into one — if it is cheap and the machine does it well, it is automatable — and what remains is a two-entry matrix:

Formative-value matrix

Low formative valueHigh formative value
Hard to automateStays human out of necessityProtected by accident — and fragile
Easy to automateAutomate without hesitationBlind spot — immediate saving, deferred cost

The bottom-right quadrant is the whole thesis. The tasks easiest to automate tend to be exactly the ones that trained whoever performed them — repetitive, low-risk, fast-feedback: precisely the properties that make a task good for training someone and good for a machine to do. Automating there is not wrong. It is an intertemporal trade-off currently decided without anyone knowing they are deciding.

Second, the cutting principle

Automate the mechanical part. Preserve a human step whenever the task teaches diagnosis, prioritisation or consequence.

And the corollary, now with causal evidence: AI should give hints, not answers. The randomised study shows the persistence cost concentrates among those who receive the direct solution[7]. This is configurable: write your own analysis before opening the assistant, ask for critique instead of solutions, alternate tasks with and without support.

Third, the AI copilot as mentor — and why it is ambiguous

Ide's model addresses this explicitly, and the answer cuts both ways[3].

In favour: copilots offer scalable access to tacit-like expertise previously acquired only through direct experience. They can partially offset the erosion, especially by assisting professionals who, as novices, failed to develop adequate skill. It is a democratisation of expertise.

Against, through two distinct mechanisms: the copilot's availability weakens the novice's incentive to invest in hands-on learning — if the answer is available, effort looks like waste. And by substituting for the learning arrangement the expert used to offer, the copilot reduces the novice's demand for that arrangement, eroding the institution of mentoring from the side of the person who would be mentored.

The reading for decision-makers: a copilot raises the floor and may lower the ceiling. It makes sense for those past the training window and for those who fell behind. It makes less sense, without restriction, for those in the window where judgment should be under construction — which is precisely where most organisations deploy it first, because that is where the productivity gain shows up fastest.

Fourth, measure proximity rather than participation

Five questions for the executive committee:

  1. Which tasks left entry-level roles over the past two years, and what did each of them train?
  2. How many hours per month does someone in training spend working alongside a person at the top of the firm's competence, in real production rather than formal training?
  3. How many people are our most capable experts training today? And three years ago?
  4. What is the time from entry to full autonomy in the role? Is it rising or falling?
  5. If half our senior people left within eighteen months, where would the replacements come from?

A warning about question 2: it is the one that matters and the only one no HR or operations system answers today. Hours of joint production are captured by nobody. Instrumenting it requires a deliberate decision — tagging co-ownership on a ticket, recording the pair on a review, logging shadowing in production. It is new work, and it is the work that separates those who will know the answer from those who will find out too late.

Who is already doing it

Brainlabs, a roughly 1,000-person U.S. media agency, went from 19 entry-level hires in October 2023 to 64 in its April 2026 cohort — a 237% increase[13]. Founder and global CEO Dan Gilbert describes the design: the company has run a training programme since it started, always training new entrants on whichever skill mattered most at the time, and since October 2023 has used those cohorts to teach people to work alongside AI from day one, rather than training the old way and bolting AI on afterwards.

Gilbert explicitly rejects the cost-cutting reading: according to him, the increase did not come from cutting juniors to save on tools, but because juniors properly trained on AI become valuable faster — a first-year strategist who can build and run an AI workflow delivers what used to require a team, which changes what the team is asked to deliver rather than shrinking it.

Caveats: this is the company's own account in a press article, with quotes edited by the journalist, without independent audit and without data on the quality of the professionals trained. A single case does not establish causation — it works as an illustration of a mechanism compatible with the model, not as a demonstration that it works.

The rule the case suggests: those who depend on repetitive tasks to train people lose both things when they automate. Those who already trained on purpose lose only the task.

06 — What remainsClosing provocation

The answer: the expert of 2035 will be whoever worked close to someone who knew, early — and that has stopped being an automatic by-product of the entry-level job and become an explicit design decision.

Many of the questions remain unanswered. We do not know whether the contraction in junior hiring is real substitution or an overreaction to expectation; the researchers themselves say they cannot test it.

The strongest alternative explanation was interest rates: the monetary tightening between 2022 and 2024 cooled hiring across the whole economy, and that alone could produce the observed pattern. The authors tested it and partly ruled it out — the most AI-exposed occupations are on average less sensitive to interest rates than the market average. That weakens the alternative explanation; it does not eliminate it. And under the strictest controls, the effect attributable to AI only becomes significant from 2024.

We also do not know what went through the mind of whoever signed the hiring freeze — only what those companies say afterwards. And studies on the Brazilian market are missing.

What is known is narrower and harder: the transfer of tacit knowledge happens through proximity, it was never in a contract, and the work that sustained it is the first to be automated. Economic theory, payroll data and a randomised experiment converge on the same dividing line — replacing the novice erodes training; augmenting the novice does not. It is the same technology, in two opposite configurations.

And here the question stops being about the market.

Your operation will run short of senior people at some point. Retirement, resignation, promotion. You probably have a mentoring programme, and it probably reports participation and completion.

Today, which companies can say how many hours per month each person in training spends exposed to business decisions grounded in human judgment or acquired experience?
