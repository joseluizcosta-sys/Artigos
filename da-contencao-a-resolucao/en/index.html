From Deflection to Resolution

What agentic AI changes — and what it doesn't — in contact center management

For a decade, service automation was measured by deflection: how many interactions the bot kept away from a human agent. That metric produced accounting savings — and the silent surrender of exhausted customers. Agentic AI inverts the logic: the system stops explaining how to solve a problem and starts executing the resolution. This paper is written for the CC/CX operations leader who must build and defend the business case for this transition. Three rules guide it: every source is ranked by how much weight its evidence carries, the central thesis is testable — and we say how to test it —, and the roadmap treats integration, not the AI model, as the real center of the project.

01 — The starting point The problem with deflection

The financial logic of customer deflection was linear: every interaction diverted from a human agent meant direct savings. The incentive was understandable — labor can represent up to 95% of a contact center's costs, according to Gartner[1].

The side effect was the design of digital mazes: deep IVRs, decision-tree bots and circular FAQs whose success was measured by the absence of a transfer to a human. Three distortions followed:

01

Deflection masked abandonment

A customer who gives up after three frustrated attempts counts as "contained" on the dashboard — and as a detractor on NPS. The metric rewarded exactly the behavior that destroys value.

02

Cost was transferred, not eliminated

Poorly resolved interactions came back as repeat calls, consumer-protection complaints and churn — costs that never appeared under "automation savings".

03

Rigidity limited the scope

Decision-tree bots failed at any compound intent ("cancel my purchase, but keep my premium subscription"). Off-script, the only options were transfer — or frustration.

02 — The anatomy of the shift What agentic AI changes

Agentic AI differs from scripted bots in two capabilities that, combined, enable end-to-end resolution: understands compound requests the way a person would — interprets intent and context in natural language, without depending on keywords or pre-mapped flows — and executes actions via tools (tool use).

Two mechanisms that are often conflated deserve separating: through function calling, the AI agent operates transactional systems — CRM, payment gateway, logistics — to execute the action, rather than describing how the customer should do it. Complementarily, RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) grounds responses in the company's knowledge base and policies, reducing hallucination. They are distinct things: RAG ensures the agent knows correctly; tool use ensures it acts.

Decision-tree bot

Informs — and only on script

Rigid "if A, then B" flows. Any intent outside the map = transfer or frustration.

Success metric: containment. The customer's problem still exists.

AI agent (agentic)

Understands — and executes

Interprets the compound intent, queries the systems and executes the action: processes the refund, adjusts the bill, reschedules the delivery.

Success metric: resolution confirmed by the absence of recontact.

The testable thesis: on transactional demands with write-level system integration, action governance and quality supervision, an AI agent raises first-contact resolution and reduces recontact without a drop in CSAT versus the equivalent human flow. If, under those conditions, recontact or CSAT worsen in a controlled pilot, the thesis is wrong — not "poorly applied".

Gartner formalized this transition in March 2025, predicting that agentic AI will autonomously resolve 80% of common customer service issues by 2029, with a 30% reduction in operational costs[2]. The firm's earlier forecast — an $80 billion reduction in contact center agent labor costs in the year 2026[1] — still referred to conversational AI; agentic AI widens automation's scope from informing to transacting.

The same Gartner predicts 80% autonomous resolution by 2029 and the cancellation of over 40% of agentic projects by 2027[10]. The forecasts don't compete: the first is the ceiling among those who survive the implementation curve; the second is the mortality along that curve. For planning purposes, the second prevails — treat the 40% as your project's base case and design to stay out of it. That's the criterion behind sections 04 and 07.

03 — The evidence, in hierarchy What the data and the real cases show — and with what weight

Before the numbers, the reading rule: not all evidence weighs the same, and mixing tiers is the most common error in the debate on AI in customer service. This paper uses three tiers and names the tier of each source as it cites it:

TIER A

Peer-reviewed study

Auditable methodology, control group, scientific publication. In this paper: the QJE study. Supports conclusions about cause and magnitude.

TIER B

Analyst-firm projection

Gartner, McKinsey. Useful for direction and order of magnitude; not measurement — forecast, with the uneven track record forecasts have.

TIER C

Company-disclosed, unaudited

Klarna, Octopus, Intercom, Sierra. Shows a mechanism is operationally possible; proves neither magnitude nor generality.

The productivity effect: AI as copilot · tier A

The most rigorous study available on generative AI in customer support — Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics — tracked 5,172 support agents with access to an AI assistant. The result: an average 15% productivity increase (issues resolved per hour), with gains concentrated among less experienced agents, who improved both speed and quality[3].

Two readings matter for managers. First: the gain is real and measurable. Second: the study measures AI as a copilot to the human, not as an autonomous agent — evidence of augmentation, not substitution. It is the operating regime of the Octopus Energy case, below.

McKinsey estimates that about 75% of generative AI's potential value concentrates in four areas, with customer operations among the main ones[4] (tier B) — which explains why the contact center became the technology's main corporate laboratory.

The Klarna case: rise and reversal · tier C

Klarna is a Swedish Buy Now, Pay Later fintech, with massive, essentially transactional customer service (refunds, returns, billing disputes) — a profile that made it agentic AI's first open laboratory at scale. In February 2024, it launched an assistant integrated with its transactional systems, covering 23 markets, 24/7, in more than 35 languages[5].

One case, three readings
Resolution at scale

2.3 million conversations in the first month — two-thirds of all service chats.

The assistant did the work equivalent of 700 full-time agents. Resolution time fell from 11 to under 2 minutes, and repeat inquiries dropped 25% — a sign of actual resolution, not deflection: the assistant didn't explain the refund policy, it processed the refund. Klarna estimated a $40 million profit improvement in 2024.

The course correction

"We focused too much on cost. The result was lower quality."

In May 2025, CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski publicly admitted the overreach — and Klarna went back to hiring human agents, after complaints about generic answers and failures in complex cases. The company moved to a hybrid model: AI for routine, high-volume demands; humans for escalations, complex cases and high-value customers.

What it establishes — and what it doesn't

The case does not prove this paper's thesis — it establishes two facts and one limit.

Fact 1: the 2024 phase shows transactional autonomous resolution at scale is operationally possible. Fact 2: the 2025 reversal marks the frontier — on complex cases, with the simultaneous cut of the human workforce, quality dropped. Limit: no public data proves the current hybrid model is superior. Treating any outcome as confirmation isn't analysis; that's why the thesis is testable, with the test defined in section 02, and the controlled pilot in the roadmap — not this case — is what tests it.

The Octopus Energy case: the supervised-copilot regime · tier C

Octopus Energy, a UK energy supplier, deployed generative AI (the Magic Ink tool, from the Kraken platform) to read, interpret and draft replies to customer emails, with access to consumption and billing data. The operating regime is predominantly copilot with human review: the AI drafts, the agent reviews and sends[8].

The published results: AI came to participate in answering about half of all incoming emails, and AI-drafted messages reached 80% customer satisfaction, against 65% for human-written replies on the same routine tasks[9].

And the case demonstrates the value of the copilot regime — consistent with the QJE study's findings[3] — more than full autonomy. It is nonetheless relevant evidence: technical precision and real-time data access produced a better experience than the generic containment reply.

The market already prices resolution · tier C

The strongest signal that this transition is structural comes from pricing models: Intercom's Fin sells via outcome-based pricing — only charging when the AI resolves, with a published average resolution rate of 76% across 12,000+ customers[11] — and Bret Taylor's Sierra literally charges per resolution delivered, capturing part of the savings of each interaction (Ramp at 90% resolved, SoFi at +33 NPS)[12][13]. When the vendor gets paid per resolution, the incentive alignment this paper proposes becomes a business model — but neither natively exposes 7-day recontact nor escalation with preserved context: those remain the responsibility of your operation's design.

04 — The core of the project Integration before the model

If this paper had to be reduced to a single practical recommendation, it would be this: an agentic AI project is an integration project that uses a model — not a model project that needs integrations. Agentic AI without reliable write-level APIs into CRM, payments and logistics is an expensive chatbot. The data and integration layer is where projects die, and it is where Gartner's 40% gets decided.

The difference between informing and executing is the difference between a read API and a write API — and writing to transactional systems brings demands the chatbot pilot never faced: a wrongly executed action is not a bad answer, it is a duplicated refund, an altered bill, a canceled delivery. Before choosing a vendor or a model, the operation must answer the minimum checklist below. Every unanswered item is scope that stays out of autonomy at go-live:

01
System inventory with write access

For each demand on the resolvability map: which read and write APIs does resolution require? Without write access there is no resolution — only a sophisticated FAQ. A demand without a mapped write API stays out of the autonomous scope.

02
Action catalog with authority limits

An explicit list of every action the agent can execute, with parameters, value limits and what requires human approval. If the catalog doesn't exist in writing, autonomy has no boundary — it has a scheduled accident.

03
Rollback and compensation

Every autonomous action needs a defined inverse action: refund of the refund, undone rescheduling, restored invoice. A transaction without a compensation path doesn't enter the autonomous scope — that is the rule separating incident from crisis.

04
API SLAs and idempotency

Latency compatible with real-time conversation (p95, not average) and idempotent calls — a retried action cannot execute twice. Also verify the agent's behavior when the API fails mid-transaction.

05
Identity and context

Customer authentication before any write action, and full context transport across channels and into human escalation. It is the technical prerequisite of the preserved-context escalation metric (section 06).

06
Sandbox and audit trail

A test environment with synthetic data to validate each action before production, and a log per executed action: what, for whom, when, under which authorization. Without a trail, there is no defense in the Air Canada scenario (section 05).

05 — The counterargument Risks and governance the enthusiasm hides

The same Gartner that projects 80% autonomous resolution by 2029 predicted, in June 2025, that over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 — due to escalating costs, unclear business value and insufficient risk controls. The firm also estimated that of the thousands of vendors claiming "agentic" capabilities, only about 130 actually have them — the rest practice agent washing: rebranded chatbots and RPA[10].

Beyond the integration bottleneck (section 04), three concrete risks for the CC/CX manager:

01
Legal liability for what the agent says and does

In 2024, a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to honor a refund policy its chatbot had invented, rejecting the argument that the bot was a "separate entity". With agentic AI the risk scales: the agent doesn't just inform incorrectly — it executes incorrectly, with immediate transactional effect.

02
Governance of autonomous actions

Which actions can the agent execute without human approval? Up to what refund amount? With what audit trail? The section 04 checklist is the technical answer; governance is the organizational one — including a named human accountable for the agent's behavior.

03
The residual-volume paradox

The more AI resolves the routine, the more complex and emotionally charged is what remains for humans — demanding more qualified, better-paid agents with better tools. Klarna's post-reversal hybrid model acknowledges exactly this.

06 — The measure The new metrics dashboard

The transition requires retiring deflection as the central KPI and adopting resolution metrics:

Klarna's 25% drop in repeat inquiries[5] is the canonical example of the metric that matters: it measures whether the problem ceased to exist, not whether the customer ceased to appear.

07 — The practice Cost, payback and implementation roadmap

No budget decision stands without the cost line. TCO has four components: consumption or license (increasingly charged per resolution — section 03); integration and data — the largest share of the investment, and the most underestimated; ongoing governance and monitoring; and reskilling of the residual team, almost always omitted from business cases.

The payback logic: the project pays for itself when (human cost per resolution − total AI cost per resolution) × migrated volume exceeds the integration investment within the defined horizon. The math is only honest if the AI cost includes repeat calls, escalations and human monitoring of the flow — the cost per resolution of section 06, not the vendor's cost per conversation.

A transparency note: this paper publishes no TCO benchmark of its own — any vendor payback number should be treated as tier C and redone with your volumes in the pilot. That is exactly what the roadmap below structures:

01
Map by resolvability, not by volume

Classify demands into three groups: viable autonomous resolution (transactional, clear rules), copilot (human judgment with AI support), pure human (complex, emotional, high value). Start with the first — and the Octopus case suggests the second delivers fast returns at low risk.

02
Run the section 04 integration checklist

Systems with write access, action catalog, rollback, API SLAs, identity and sandbox — before choosing a model or vendor. An unanswered item is scope outside autonomy at go-live.

03
Pilot with a control group

AI flow vs. human flow on the same demand, measuring the section 06 dashboard — especially 7-day recontact. It is the instrument that tests section 02's thesis in your operation; without a counterfactual, the result is an opinion.

04
Reinvest in the residual human

Resize the profile, seniority and compensation of the team that will handle complex cases. Guarantee escalation with full context — and put that cost in the TCO from the start.

05
Test the vendor against agent washing

Demand a demonstration of real transactional execution in your systems, in a sandbox, before signing. "Agentic" in the sales deck is not evidence — and neither is a proposal without an open cost per resolution.

Conclusion The ROI that matters

The question that defined the last decade — "how many calls did we avoid?" — measured a company's ability to hide from its customers. The question that defines the next one — "how many problems did we resolve without friction?" — measures its ability to serve them.

Agentic AI makes the second question technically answerable at scale. But the evidence examined — weighed by its tier — imposes a condition: the technology amplifies the service design it finds. Applied as cost-cutting without quality supervision, it degrades the experience with unprecedented efficiency. Applied on top of well-designed flows — transactional integration solved, action governance, resolution metrics and a controlled pilot that can prove the thesis wrong — it converts the contact center from a squeezable cost center into a retention asset.

The real ROI is not in the calls we avoided. It is in the customers who stay because their problems ceased to exist in seconds — and in the humans, more qualified, handling what the machine cannot yet reach.
