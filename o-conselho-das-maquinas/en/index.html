The Council of Machines

How to put the judgment — and the biases — of AI models to work for you when making decisions

Over a single weekend, Andrej Karpathy — one of the most influential AI researchers of the past decade, an OpenAI co-founder and Tesla's former AI director — built an app where several AI models read, critique and vote on one another's answers. The result is less a technical curiosity than an uncomfortable mirror: the machines have preferences, have biases — and, left to themselves, tend to side with the most "popular" one. The value of the experiment isn't in who tops the ranking, but in the method it offers for hard decisions.

01 — The Origin The Origin Story

Who he is

Andrej Karpathy

AI researcher · b. Bratislava, 1986

One of the most influential names in deep learning. He created and taught a course at Stanford — the university's first major deep learning course — and has a habit of turning complex ideas into open, didactic tools.

  • 2015PhD at Stanford, advised by Fei-Fei Li. Founding member of OpenAI.
  • 2017–22Director of AI and Autopilot Vision at Tesla.
  • 2024Founds Eureka Labs, an AI education platform.
  • 2026Joins Anthropic — the maker of Claude.

The irony didn't go unnoticed: the council he built voted Claude the worst model, months before he went to work at Anthropic, the company that makes Claude.

The LLM Council started as a low-key experiment. Karpathy built the project over a single Saturday using "vibe coding"iAI-guided programming, where the developer describes the intent in natural language and lets the model write the code. Karpathy himself popularized the term. — AI-guided programming — and published the open repository on GitHub, sharing the early results on X (formerly Twitter). In his own words, the app was "99% vibe coded".

The question was simple and provocative: what happens if artificial intelligences stop merely answering humans and start evaluating one another? Karpathy used the council for a very concrete use case — reading book chapters side by side with several models and comparing the quality of the readings.

The point wasn't to build a product. It was to observe a behavior: AIs that, instead of competing for human approval, are forced to judge one another's work under explicit rules.

02 — The Result The Machines' Verdict

Karpathy's account reveals something counterintuitive: AIs are surprisingly capable and willing to admit when a rival model's answer is better than their own. Reading chapters and scoring answers, the council consistently crowned OpenAI's GPT-5.1 as the best and sharpest — while Claude was just as consistently picked as the worst, deemed "dry" and too terse in that domain. Gemini 3 and Grok 4 landed in the middle.

But the experiment's most honest finding is the author's own caveat. Karpathy made clear that the verdict is subjective and that he doesn't agree 100% with the machines: qualitatively, he found GPT-5.1 sometimes verbose and too "spread out", at times preferring Gemini 3's more condensed synthesis.

"The models consistently pick GPT-5.1 as the best and Claude as the worst. I'm not 100% convinced this matches my own qualitative assessment." — Andrej Karpathy, on X (November 2025)

This contradiction between the machines' preference and the creator's is the method's first warning sign: a council of evaluators doesn't deliver the truth — it delivers a consensus. And consensus, as we'll see, carries biases of its own that are eliminated only in how the system is designed — not by asking the model for good judgment.

03 — The Mechanics How the Council Works

The model runs in a three-stage cycle, designed to extract intelligence by combining competition and consensus. Each stage has a distinct function — and the order matters.

STAGE 01

Independent Generation

The question is sent simultaneously to several models. Answers are generated side by side, without any model seeing another's — then anonymized as "Answer A", "Answer B", etc.

STAGE 02

Anonymous Peer Review

Each model reads all rival answers — without knowing who wrote them — and rates them for accuracy and insight: which argument is strongest, which has the biggest blind spot, and what everyone missed.

STAGE 03

The Chairman's Synthesis

A "Chairman" model takes over. It doesn't generate new ideas: it compiles where there's agreement (high confidence), where there's legitimate disagreement, the blind spots revealed — and delivers a clear verdict with the next step.

The LLM Council cycle — from question to verdict

Models and ranking reflect the council Karpathy configured in Nov 2025. The anonymity lock in Stage 02 is what keeps the system from becoming a popularity contest.

The most important engineering detail is in stage 02. Anonymization isn't cosmetic: it exists precisely to stop a model from "playing to the crowd" and favoring the one it recognizes. It's the piece that separates an honest evaluation from a popularity contest — and, as we'll see next, it's also the exact point where the system most easily breaks.

04 — The Risk The Submission Bias

Here is the most fascinating — and at the same time most dangerous — finding. When the anonymity stage is removed — when the models know whose answer is whose — a "submission bias" effect is reported: models like Claude or Gemini would tend to "give up" their own logic and correct themselves to agree with GPT's answer, simply because they recognize the source.

Critical note. We should treat this as a hypothesis, not a fact. What is well documented in the literature is only the underlying mechanism: sycophancy (the tendency to agree with the apparently endorsed position) and self-preference / familiarity bias in LLMs used as judges. These two effects lend support to the "submission bias" — but an indication isn't proof. To elevate it from hypothesis to fact, you'd need to run the same set of answers with and without anonymity, across several rounds, and measure whether — and how much — the scores change when the model's identity is revealed.

The practical consequence is direct and doesn't depend on the hypothesis being true: the anonymization in stage 02 is a risk control. Remove it and you swap a council of peers for an echo chamber that converges on the most "prestigious" model. For anyone who intends to use the method on real decisions, the rule is non-negotiable: answers must be evaluated blind. It's the model-world equivalent of double-blind review in science.

05 — The Application Where the Council Shines

The LLM Council is a terrible tool for factual questions. "What's the capital of France?" doesn't need a council — it needs a search. The value shows up in exactly the opposite territory: high-uncertainty scenarios, where there's no obvious right answer and the cost of deciding wrong is high. In those cases, the divergence between models isn't noise — it's information about where the risks are.

Three real contact center decisions where the council adds value
Strategy

"Do I reinforce the human team for the demand peak, or invest first in automating service?"

There's no single answer — it depends on volume, seasonality, cost per contact and operational maturity. The council forces you to make explicit the trade-offs of each path (fixed headcount cost vs. the cost and time to deploy AI) and reveals the blind spot a single AI would hide by "choosing" too quickly.

Experience

At what point should the bot hand off to a human — and what should it say before handing off?

Evaluate the service flow before putting it into production. Peer review works as a cheap pre-test: it flags where the script creates frustration, which answer becomes a legal or reputational risk, and the missing escalation trigger — before a real customer pays to find out.

Operations

"Do I keep service in-house or outsource to a BPO — and in what order do I make the transition?"

Structural decisions and drastic pivots. The council organizes the case for and against each path, separating what is consensus (proceed with confidence) from what is legitimate dispute (where it's worth pausing and measuring before committing the operation).

The evidence that the method works

Karpathy's council is too new to have a published operational case. But the mechanism behind it — several models that read each other before concluding — has already been measured. In Mixture-of-Agents (ICLR 2025), a council of open-source models reached a 65.1% win rate on the AlpacaEval 2.0 benchmark, beating GPT-4 Omni alone (57.5%). And in Multiagent Debate (Du et al., ICML 2024), forcing the models to debate before answering raised factuality and reasoning above any single model.

The honest caveat comes from the same field: the Self-MoA study showed that, across several tests, a single top-tier model — queried multiple times with its best answers combined — beats the council of different models. The right reading isn't "more models is always better", but that the gain comes from structured deliberation, not from the number of brands at the table — sometimes a single model wearing several lenses is enough.

Translated to operations: the question the method improves isn't "automate or not", but where automation destroys value — the boundary between the repetitive contact, which AI handles well and cheaply, and the high-friction contact, where a poorly calibrated escalation turns into a repeat call, legal risk and brand erosion. Costs that don't show up on the cost-per-contact spreadsheet in the month of the decision, only the following quarter. A single AI, optimistic by nature, tends to underweight that risk; a council, forced to diverge, puts it on the table.

The pattern is clear: the council doesn't replace the decision-maker — it improves the quality of the question before the decision. It delivers a map of agreements, divergences and blind spots. The final choice remains human; what changes is that it's now made with the terrain lit up.

06 — The Genealogy The Parallel with De Bono

There's a more precise way to understand the LLM Council than "several AIs chatting": it's the structured digitization of Edward de Bono's method. In Six Thinking Hats (1985), De Bono proposes human parallel thinking — separating perspectives into "hats" so the group looks at the same problem from one angle at a time, without ego and debate stalling the analysis.

The council operationalizes exactly that, swapping hats for specialized advisors. The mapping is almost one-to-one:

De Bono — Black Hat

The Contrarian

Focus on what can go wrong: risks, failures, objections. In the council, it's the advisor tasked with hunting the blind spot and the argument that doesn't hold up.

De Bono — Yellow Hat

The Expansionist

Focus on maximizing the upside: benefits, opportunities, the best case. In the council, it's the one who widens the option space instead of closing the decision too early.

And the parallel culminates in the most important function of both systems — orchestration. De Bono's Blue Hat and Karpathy's Chairman play an identical role: they don't generate new ideas, they organize the chaos of competing perspectives, remove the biases and ensure all voices culminate in a final decision that is practical and smart.

"The genius, in both models, isn't in any single advisor. It's in whoever orchestrates — the Blue in De Bono, the Chairman in Karpathy." — Author's synthesis

That's where the transferable lesson lies, regardless of which model wins the ranking of the day: good decisions rarely come from a single voice, however brilliant. They come from a process that forces perspectives to diverge before converging — and from someone, human or machine, with the discipline to separate consensus from echo. The LLM Council is, at bottom, the old wisdom of parallel thinking dressed up as software. What it adds isn't the idea. It's the ability to run it on demand, at any hour, over any hard decision.

A distinction makes the parallel even sharper. De Bono doesn't switch people for each hat — it's the same mind taking on, one at a time, a different role. The version of the council closest to that doesn't use distinct models, but a single model wearing several lenses: the same "brain" instructed to think now as the Contrarian, now as the Expansionist. And there's a gain the multi-model version doesn't have — when the advisor is always the same model, the submission bias simply doesn't exist: there's no rival brand to defer to, because all voices come from the same source. What varies is the role, not the prestige.

The cost of that choice is the flip side of its advantage: a single model carries correlated blind spots — every lens inherits the same base biases, and the "disagreement" may be more role-play than real divergence. The practical rule that emerges is direct: to force perspectives (what De Bono does best) and escape the herd effect, a single model with several lenses usually suffices — it's cheaper and faster; when what's at stake demands genuine diversity of reasoning, it's worth paying the price of orchestrating rival models and living with the identity bias they bring back.

Hands-on Run your own AI council

If the topic caught your interest, the most useful path isn't to read more about the council — it's to put it to work and then adapt it to your own decision. Three steps, in increasing depth:

01

Experiment

~15 min

Run the council yourself. Karpathy's original repository runs locally and fans out to several models via OpenRouter (you need an API key): karpathy/llm-council. If you already use Claude Code, there are Markdown versions that reproduce the cycle within a single session, with subagents as advisors — for example ngmeyer/council-review. Start with a real decision, not a test.

02

Understand the foundation

1 read

The council didn't come out of nowhere — it operationalizes peer-reviewed research. To understand why cross-model evaluation works and where it fails, read Zheng et al., "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge" (NeurIPS 2023): it's the work that formally documents the position and verbosity biases of LLM judges — exactly the grounding for this article's critical note. arXiv:2306.05685. For the model-debate side, go to Du, Tenenbaum & Mordatch, Multiagent Debate (ICML 2024). Link.

03

Apply it to your context

playbook

The real value appears when you adapt the method to a concrete decision — say, in your contact center. A minimal playbook to avoid misusing it:

  1. Frame a decision, not a factual question. Make the cost of being wrong explicit. If the question has a single answer, it doesn't need a council.
  2. Define the advisors as lenses. Instead of five models, five angles: the Contrarian (risks), the Expansionist (gains), the Operational one (cost and deployment). It's De Bono digitized.
  3. Guarantee anonymity in the evaluation. Without that lock, the council becomes a popularity contest. Evaluate the answers blind.
  4. Read the verdict skeptically. Use consensus as a confidence signal and divergence as a risk map — but the final decision stays yours, not the chairman's.
