Algorithmic Monocultures in Hiring

When a handful of vendors supply the algorithms that screen nearly everyone, the same people get rejected everywhere — not by chance, but because the systems decide in correlated ways.

The problem

Few vendors, one verdict

Over 90% of employers use automated screening, and the market concentrates in very few vendors. A single logic ends up guarding many gates.

Algorithmic monoculture

“When the same algorithm dominates a sector — or algorithms built in similar ways, on similar data, make similar decisions.”

— Kathleen A. Creel, co-author

Over 60% of Fortune 100 companies use the algorithms of a single video-screening vendor. The study examines another case at comparable scale: the platform pymetrics (acquired by Harver in 2022), with 42 distinct models — “gamified” assessments that measure behavioral traits instead of asking for a résumé.

The study

The largest empirical study of algorithmic hiring

The authors are the first group to independently analyze hiring algorithms actually in production, at scale — a level of access almost no outsider has obtained.

The method

Measuring position by position reveals what averages hid

The decisive methodological choice: assess adverse impact per position (as Title VII and the four-fifths rule require), not in aggregate. That is where the disparity surfaces.

Aggregate analysis

“No measurable bias”

Blending all positions together, prior studies — and audits published by the vendor itself — found little or no adverse impact.

Position-by-position analysis

Disparity exposed

Position by position, the discrimination the average diluted reappears — aligned with the legal standard that evaluates each job in isolation.

The finding

The monoculture has a color

Without collecting demographic data, the games' behavioral proxies end up acting as racial proxies — and the bill falls heaviest on Black and Asian applicants.

What to do

Treat concentration as systemic risk

If a single algorithm can reject someone across the whole market, oversight has to watch the market — not just each employer.

Measure adverse impact per position

Assess adverse impact position by position, to the Title VII standard — not in aggregate averages that hide the disparity.

Market-level surveillance

Federal agencies should quantify the rate of homogeneous outcomes across employers that share the same system.

Monitor concentrated dependencies

Track how much of the market relies on a single vendor, to anticipate correlated failures.

Open access for researchers

Guarantee independent data access — today, this was the only group to empirically study a deployed system at scale.
