The Error
That Strengthens

How AI contact centers get stronger every time they fail

In the traditional view, a service error is a cost — it creates rework, churn, and frustration. The most resilient operations treat it differently: every failure carries information that makes the system smarter the next time around.

Context Three bots, three failures, the same management error

Between 2022 and 2025, three companies watched their support chatbots fail in public. The errors were different — an invented policy, a hallucinated rule, a bot that trashed its own brand. The management error was the same: none of the three had a mechanism to turn that failure into learning before it became a loss. Each case hits a different front of damage.

Three real failures — three types of damage
Legal damage

The bot invented a refund policy (2022–2024).

The chatbot told a customer he could claim the bereavement fare up to 90 days after his flight — a policy that didn't exist. Air Canada denied the refund and, in court, argued the bot was "a separate legal entity." In February 2024, British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal rejected the argument and found the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation. The award was small — the real cost was becoming a legal precedent cited worldwide.

Revenue damage

The bot hallucinated a rule and triggered cancellations (April 2025).

After a session bug logged users out, the support agent "Sam" replied that this was "expected behavior" under a new one-device-per-subscription policy. The policy didn't exist — the bot invented it and presented it as official. Worse: it gave different answers to the same question. Developers with multi-device workflows read the rule and canceled. The co-founder had to apologize publicly.

Reputational damage

The bot swore and trashed its own brand (January 2024).

A customer chasing a lost parcel got DPD's chatbot to swear, write a poem calling itself "useless," and declare that "DPD is the worst delivery service in the world." The screenshots went viral — 1.3 million views in hours. DPD disabled the AI the same day, blaming a system update.

Signal

The same error — treated as information.

In each case, the first occurrence was a cheap signal. Air Canada could have caught the bot's first wrong answer and fixed the policy before the lawsuit. Cursor had a clear symptom — the bot giving different answers to the same question — flagging hallucination days before the cancellations. DPD had the jailbreak sitting in a log before it went viral. All three lacked the same mechanism: the failure feeding the next cycle so it never repeats. The cost of capturing the signal was near zero; the cost of ignoring it was a lawsuit, churn, and a PR crisis.

This is the turning point that separates two kinds of operation. The fragile one treats the error as an anomaly to deny — closes the ticket, externalizes the blame, and the same error reappears next week with another customer. The resilient one treats the same error as information: every failure carries data about where the environment changed, and that data makes the system smarter next time.

Contact centers have historically been built for maximum efficiency: no redundancies, minimal idle time, extreme standardization. It seemed rational. But complex systemsiAn interdisciplinary field studying how systems made of many interconnected components produce unpredictable emergent behaviors. The foundation of the Cynefin framework and Taleb's thinking. that depend on human behavior cannot be fully predicted — and an operation that works perfectly under normal conditions and collapses at the first deviation is not efficient. It is a systemic risk waiting to materialize. The pages that follow are about how to invert this: making the error strengthen the system instead of breaking it.

01 — Errors as Signals The Nutritive Error*

Systems designed only for the predictable treat out-of-pattern variations as anomalies to be eliminated. Systems that learn do the opposite: they use those variations as raw material. When an AI fails to understand a new expression or a shift in customer behavior, it is not just failing — it is revealing a change in the environment that no dashboard had anticipated.

The same error — two possible responses
Cost

AI fails to understand the request.

Automatic fallback to a human agent. The ticket is closed as "not understood." The model learns nothing. The same error repeats next week — and the week after — until it becomes a scale problem.

Signal

AI fails to understand the request.

Fallback to a human agent — but the failure is logged as a symptom of environmental change. The pattern of misunderstanding feeds the next training cycle. Today's error prevents tomorrow's systemic failure.

Three mechanisms make this concrete:

01

Anomaly Detection as Signal

Every AI comprehension failure is data, not noise. It shows where the environment changed before any traditional metric does.

02

Digital Hormesis

In biology, small doses of stress make organisms stronger — large doses kill them. Applied to AI flows, this means deliberately introducing controlled variations to test resilience before real stress arrives.

03

Error-Learning Architecture

Train the model primarily on cases where satisfaction scores were lowest. The worst customer moments carry the richest information.

The result is a system with feedback loopsiFeedback loop — the time between an error occurring and the system correcting itself. The shorter the loop, the more antifragile the process. short enough that errors are corrected almost as soon as they occur. Not as an exception — as design.

02 — Optionality in Processes Freedom by Design

Optionality is the ability to benefit from the upside of an event without being destroyed by the downside. In service processes, this means one thing: escape from deterministic flows.

Rigid "if A then B" flowcharts are concave — they work well under normal conditions and collapse under variation. A process with optionality is convex: it has multiple available paths, and the cost of switching to an alternative route is always lower than the cost of stopping entirely.

"Optionality works by negative information — reducing the space of what we do by knowledge of what does not work." — Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Antifragile (2012)

In process design, concave means it works well under normal conditions but gets significantly worse when something goes off-script. Convex means it works reasonably under normal conditions — and actually improves when something unexpected happens.

Concave Process (Fragile)

Works well under normal conditions — gets much worse when something goes off-script.

One error stops everything

System outage, a business rule that doesn't apply, or a scenario outside the standard flow.

Result: 100% of operations halt. Unlimited risk, no escape valve.

Convex Process (Resilient)

Works reasonably under normal conditions — actually improves when something unexpected happens.

One error opens alternatives

AI detects emotional complexity or a technical block and immediately routes to a specialist.

Cost of the detour: fixed and predictable. Gain from avoiding a PR crisis: unlimited.

The most important implication is not operational — it is about design: AI should not just make decisions. It should generate options. Instead of executing a single path, the system presents the human manager or agent three possible routes, each with its risk assessment. The final decision stays human; the AI expands the space of possibilities.

03 — The Hydra Effect† in CX When the system grows where it is cut

In Greek mythology, the Hydra was a creature that generated two heads every time one was cut off. A CX system that learns from errors and builds in multiple response paths works the same way: every eliminated failure point strengthens the system rather than merely removing it.

Three properties emerge from this combination:

01
No Single Point of Failure

Having multiple available paths ensures no individual breakdown paralyzes the process. If one path closes, another opens — by design, not improvisation.

02
Volatility as Knowledge

Every unexpected behavior or process variation is a training data point. The AI model becomes more sophisticated as the environment becomes less predictable — not despite it, but because of it.

03
Decentralized Decision-Making

Agents — human or AI — with the freedom to choose between paths make better decisions than those locked into static manuals. The intelligence of the system is not at the top of the hierarchy; it is distributed across every point of contact.

The most efficient system is the one that learns fastest when the unexpected happens — and the one that kept alternative paths open while it learned.

04 — The Limits The Errors That Aren't Nutritive

Not every error strengthens. This is the line that separates resilience from recklessness. Learning from failure only works while the failure is survivable: no single event can be allowed to take the operation out of the game. The Air Canada case cost little in money and still became public learning — it was reversible. Others are not.

Reversible error (nutritive)

The AI misses slang, a flow stalls, a response falls below expectations.

Limited cost, recoverable, rich in information. It should be catalogued — it's the raw material of learning.

Ruin error (irreversible)

Data breach, wrong regulatory or legal guidance, an undue contractual promise, viral damage.

Disproportionate, sometimes terminal cost. It must be eliminated by design, with a hard barrier — not learned by trial.

The practical rule: optionality and error-learning apply in the domain of the reversible. In the domain of ruin, the inverse principle holds — hard rules and protection. A mature system doesn't embrace every error; it knows precisely which error it can embrace and which it must shield against.

In practice this becomes a simple governance rule: reversible errors can ship and be learned from; ruin errors are blocked by design — the bot cannot, by configuration, state a refund, legal, or contractual policy. That is exactly the guardrail Air Canada and Cursor lacked.

It's worth being precise about the concept's limit: the antifragility described here applies to the domain of the reversible — that's where the error becomes raw material and the system gains from volatility. Ruin doesn't call for antifragility; it calls for classic robustness, variance reduction, and deterministic barriers. The two principles don't compete — they operate in different domains, and maturity lies in knowing where each one applies.

05 — The Measure If you can't measure it, you can't manage it

Resilience that isn't measured becomes rhetoric. To move from metaphor to management, the gain needs indicators that tie learning to the customer outcome — not just to the AI's internal activity. Four make the concept auditable, and any manager can score their own operation against them:

01
Healing time

The interval between an error occurring and the fix reaching production. The master indicator: measure it in days, not quarters.

02
Recurrence rate of the same error

Of the catalogued failures, what share happens again after a training cycle? A system that learns sees this number fall consistently.

03
Repeat contact on already-catalogued failure modes

Does the AI resolve as well (or better) on the failure modes it has already fixed as it does on average? It's the one test that makes antifragility falsifiable: if the system got stronger where it was hurt, repeat contact on those modes falls below baseline.

04
Cost per resolved contact

The outcome anchor. Fast healing is worthless if every captured "signal" adds a human step and cost per resolution climbs. It measures whether the learning is efficient — or merely expensive.

The decisive test isn't cataloguing more errors — it's proving that the already-healed failure modes resolve as well as baseline, at a stable cost per contact. If they do, the system genuinely got stronger where it was hurt. If not, you built a logging system, not a learning one.

06 — The Cost Convexity isn't free

Optionality has a side that enthusiasm hides: every alternative path preserved carries a cost of maintenance, testing, and governance. Multiple flows no one monitors aren't resilience — they're technical debt and new failure points in disguise. Real convexity demands discipline on three fronts: active pruning (a path that is never triggered should be removed, not accumulated), a variance ceiling (deliberate stress injection happens only in an isolated environment, never on the real customer who affects NPS and churn), and an explicit cost per option (each route enters with a known price, reviewed against the loss it prevents).

Optionality only pays off while the cost of keeping the option is provably lower than the cost of not having it when the crisis hits. When that relationship inverts — and it inverts silently — what looked like resilience becomes overhead. Mature design isn't the one that creates the most options; it's the one that knows which options are worth paying to keep.
